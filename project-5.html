SAP ERP Implementation & BPM Integration

Role: Project Manager  |  Scope: ERP (SAP MM/FI/CO) and BPM integration  |  Departments: Accounting, Procurement

Business Context & Problem

The company's existing ERP system was outdated and could no longer keep up with the growing complexity of accounting and procurement workflows, so an ERP modernization project was launched to bring in SAP (MM/FI/CO modules) as a replacement. Because the company also ran a BPM system for internal approvals and operational workflows, this wasn't simply a system swap — it also had to guarantee that data and process logic between BPM and the new SAP system connected correctly, without creating a new information silo or duplicate work.

My Role & Key Challenges

My role: Project Manager, spanning the full project lifecycle — from requirements interviews and process analysis, to planning the BPM/SAP system integration, to coordinating UAT and integration testing, through to post-go-live system maintenance and improvement.

Stakeholders

  • Accounting department (finance process owner)
  • Procurement department (purchasing process owner)
  • IT / systems integration team

Constraints

  • Timeline pressure to complete requirements gathering, integration, and testing within a limited window
  • Needed to coordinate requirements and priorities across multiple departments at once

Risks

  • Accounting and procurement staff were unfamiliar with SAP, making user adoption a decisive factor in the rollout's success
  • Large differences in fields and process logic between BPM and SAP made data mapping and integration design difficult

Approach & Solution

The project followed a full path from requirements to production: first, requirements interviews with accounting and procurement to identify the gap between existing processes and the SAP MM/FI/CO modules; then planning the integration approach between BPM and SAP and designing the data integration flow between the two systems; then validating correctness through integration testing and UAT; and finally ongoing system maintenance and process improvement after go-live.

Given that accounting and procurement staff weren't naturally familiar with SAP, the requirements interviews weren't just about gathering specs — they also served as a communication and education channel, helping users gradually understand the logic behind the new process and lowering resistance and learning cost once the system actually went live.

Key Decisions & Trade-offs

Decision

Prioritized getting accounting and procurement's core workflows (requisitions, payment requests, GL posting, etc.) fully correct for go-live, and planned secondary or edge-case functionality to be refined iteratively after launch.

Why

Accounting and procurement are the most fundamental, least-forgiving parts of daily operations — if the core workflow broke at go-live, it would disrupt the company's entire operating rhythm. Compared with chasing full feature coverage, locking down a stable, trustworthy core process first was the lower-risk approach.

What we gave up

Some non-core features and process refinements had to wait until after go-live, meaning users needed some temporary workarounds in the short term — in exchange for the core finance and procurement processes standing on solid ground from day one.

Deliverables & Impact

Deliverables

  • SAP MM/FI/CO module implementation, replacing the legacy ERP system
  • An integration design and data flow between BPM and SAP
  • Requirements specifications and test cases for the accounting and procurement departments
  • A post-go-live system maintenance and process improvement mechanism

Impact

  • SAP MM/FI/CO went live successfully, with accounting and procurement's core workflows fully transitioned from the legacy system to the new ERP
  • Established a working data integration flow between BPM and SAP, preventing the two systems from operating as separate silos
  • Lowered user resistance to the new system through structured interviews and communication, speeding up department-level adoption

Lessons Learned

The hard part of an ERP rollout is rarely the system itself — it's whether users can actually adopt and use it. Accounting and procurement's unfamiliarity with SAP meant requirements interviews couldn't be a one-way spec-gathering exercise; they needed real effort spent communicating and explaining why the new process was designed the way it was. With limited time and resources, locking down the correctness of the core workflow first and leaving secondary refinements for post-launch iteration was more pragmatic — and more likely to deliver on time and reliably — than trying to get everything perfect in one shot.
